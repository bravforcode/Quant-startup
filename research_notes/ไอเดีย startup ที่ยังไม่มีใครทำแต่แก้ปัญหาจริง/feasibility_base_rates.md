# Feasibility and Base Rates for Startup Ideas (evidence as of 2026-10-20)

Scope note: this file covers the six key questions. Several requested items (YC data, Kauffman/Shane, Startup Genome 2024+ data, sector-level time-to-revenue, regulatory-breakthrough case studies, "obvious but undone" case studies) were not verified within this research pass and are listed under Gaps. Sources are dated where the dates were visible. Secondary sites that quote BLS or CB Insights numbers are flagged, and where the primary page was checked, its numbers are used.

## Key Question 1: What do credible datasets say about failure rates, time to revenue, and sector outcomes?

### Takeaway
The best-documented failure data is fragmented across incompatible units (VC-backed companies vs. all employer establishments vs. 2011 high-growth tech startups), so no single "startup failure rate" is defensible. Reported causes of failure are dominated by product-market fit and cash-related issues, but the headline percentages depend heavily on which CB Insights report is quoted.

### Cited Findings
- **CB Insights, current report (updated 2026-03-06):** analyzed 431 VC-backed companies that publicly shut down since 2023 (excluding prior exits); reasons could be identified for 385. "Ran out of capital" is cited in 70% of cases but is described by CB Insights as "almost always the final cause of death"; poor product-market fit 43%, bad timing 29%, unsustainable unit economics 19%. Multiple reasons per company, so percentages exceed 100%. [CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/)
- **CB Insights, same report:** the 431 companies raised $17.5B combined; median equity raised $11M, average $48M. Median time from last fundraise to death was 22 months; roughly a quarter were "walking dead" for more than 3 years after their last raise. Two-thirds of shrinking-headcount cases were shrinking in the 6 months before death. [CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/)
- **CB Insights sector split (same report):** healthcare and biotech 62 companies (14%), $5.1B destroyed; fintech 57 companies (13%) with median raise of only $4M and 60% failures outside the US; food and agriculture 54 companies (13%), roughly a third alt-protein/cultivated meat. [CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/)
- **Conflicting older CB Insights figure:** the widely quoted "no market need, 42%" finding comes from an earlier CB Insights post-mortem analysis (secondary sites cite 100+ to 400+ post-mortems depending on source). The current primary CB Insights page does not reproduce that 42% figure; its own top-reason figures are PMF 43% of the 2023+ cohort. Secondary sources conflict on sample size (one says 400+ post-mortems, another 431) and should not be cited as the original study without checking. [CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/); secondary: [Segmentos](https://segmentos.io/blog/why-startups-fail), [UnicornScreener](https://unicornscreener.vc/blog/why-startups-fail-what-the-data-actually-shows)
- **Startup Genome (2011, dated):** dataset of over 3,200 high-growth technology/internet startups. Found 70% "scaled prematurely" along at least one dimension; no prematurely-scaled startup passed 100,000 users; 93% of prematurely-scaled startups never exceeded $100k revenue per month; properly scaled startups grew about 20 times faster. Startup Genome also says market size, product release cycles, education, gender, and founder experience had no influence on whether a company scaled prematurely. [Startup Genome deep dive, Sept 2011](https://startupgenome.com/insights/premature-scaling-a-deep-dive); full report PDF: [Startup Genome Report Extra](https://s3.amazonaws.com/startupcompass-public/StartupGenomeReport2_Why_Startups_Fail_v2.pdf)
- **Startup Genome headline inconsistency:** the report text in the deep dive says 70% of startups "possess" premature scaling, while the PDF and secondary sites say "74% of high-growth internet startups fail due to premature scaling." These are different claims (incidence vs. attributed cause of failure). The 74% figure should be cited only with the PDF as source and the caveat that it is an attributed cause from one 2011 dataset. [PDF](https://s3.amazonaws.com/startupcompass-public/StartupGenomeReport2_Why_Startups_Fail_v2.pdf); secondary: [Duet Partners](https://www.duetpartners.com/why-is-premature-scaling-still-the-biggest-startup-killer/)
- **Carta shutdown counts (secondary):** a LinkedIn post attributed to Carta data reports 769 shutdowns in 2023 and 966 in 2024, described as "accelerating in 2025." I could not confirm these numbers on a Carta primary page in this pass. [LinkedIn post citing Carta](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/diaminate_startups-entrepreneurship-founderlife-activity-7361396146688147456-Be8V)
- **Carta, State of Startups 2025 (primary, 2025 data):** analysis of more than 60,000 startups and 3,000 venture funds on Carta; AI startups captured 44% of all US startup capital; initial equity grants shrank 50% since late 2022. The page is a gated summary; these figures are from the landing text only. [Carta State of Startups 2025](https://carta.com/data/resources/state-of-startups-2025/). Carta's own dataset is its customer base, so it is not representative of all startups.
- **BLS establishment survival (all new employer establishments, not VC startups):** via a secondary summary of BLS Business Employment Dynamics: 77.9% of new establishments survive year one, 51.4% survive five years (March 2020 cohort, observed March 2025), and 49.3% is the average five-year survival across cohorts since 1994 (range 45.4% to 51.9%). Ten-year survival in the 32.4% to 35.3% band. [startbusinessbystate.com summary of BLS BED](https://startbusinessbystate.com/business-survival-rates-by-state/); primary: [BLS Establishment Age and Survival Data](https://www.bls.gov/bdm/bdmage.htm) (page title and structure confirmed; the numbers above were NOT verified on the primary page because the full scrape failed).
- **Conflicting BLS figures in secondary sources:** another summary gives 79.6% (year one), 50.6% (year five), and 34.7% (year ten), for a different cohort window. Both attribute to BLS BED. The two summaries are not reconcilable from this pass; use the primary BLS page before quoting any specific number. [The Broker Shop](https://thebrokershopinc.com/small-business-survival-rate-by-industry.html)
- **Census BDS (secondary):** about 45% of new employer establishments survive to year five across NAICS industries (cited as Census BDS, 2023). Not verified on a Census primary page. [VantaInsights](https://vantainsights.com/insights/business-failure-rate)

### Inferences
- Across these sources, roughly half of new employer establishments are gone within five years, and VC-backed companies fail for a mix of demand and cash reasons. The base rates above are not directly comparable to VC-backed startup survival, which is not measured in these sources.
- CB Insights's own framing ("ran out of capital" is the last step, PMF and unit economics are the root) suggests the scoring framework should weight demand evidence and unit economics ahead of raise size.
- Sector differences in the CB Insights 2023+ cohort (healthcare/biotech by capital destroyed, fintech by count and emerging-market concentration, food/agri by alt-protein unwind) are real for that cohort, but they reflect 2021-2022 capital cycles more than structural survival rates. No sector-level survival rate for VC-backed startups was verified.

### Gaps
- Time to first revenue by sector: not found in any verified source. Startup Genome's 2011 "scaling" findings are the only time-related figures, and they are about growth stage, not time to revenue.
- Primary BLS page numbers not verified (full scrape failed; search snippet confirmed page exists).
- Carta shutdown counts not verified on a Carta primary page.
- A survival study of VC-backed startups by sector (e.g., Kauffman, Shane, or peer-reviewed cohort studies) was not retrieved in this pass.
- Y Combinator: no verified YC outcome data was retrieved.

## Key Question 2: Which defensibility factors have worked for 2020-2026 startups, and which have stopped working because of AI commoditization?

### Takeaway
The verified evidence on current defensibility is thin. The main credible-sounding claim (data moats are weaker than assumed) is a 2019 a16z essay, which is practitioner opinion. The only data-backed evidence in this pass is that AI startups absorb a large share of capital (Carta 2025) and that at least one AI company (Builder.ai) collapsed after overstated revenue, which speaks to diligence rather than moat structure.

### Cited Findings
- a16z (Martin Casado, 2019), "The Empty Promise of Data Moats": argues that data advantages are often overstated because data often has diminishing returns on model quality and that the edge cases become increasingly costly. **Practitioner opinion, not an empirical study.** [a16z, The Empty Promise of Data Moats](https://a16z.com/the-empty-promise-of-data-moats/) (original date 2019-05-09 per [a16z snippet](https://a16z.com/the-new-business-of-ai-and-how-its-different-from-traditional-software/))
- a16z, "The New Business of AI (and How It's Different From Traditional Software)": states that "the playbook for defending AI businesses is still being written," and that as models mature, each new edge case costs more to address while serving fewer customers. **Opinion, undated in the excerpt.** [a16z](https://a16z.com/the-new-business-of-ai-and-how-its-different-from-traditional-software/)
- Carta 2025: AI startups captured 44% of US startup capital. This shows investor concentration, not defensibility. [Carta](https://carta.com/data/resources/state-of-startups-2025/)
- Builder.ai (secondary reporting): once valued at over $1B; reported 2024 revenue of about $220M was found to be roughly $55M in an internal investigation; insolvency in May 2025. Secondary sources only (mlq.ai, Emergent). Shows that "AI" claims and revenue quality need verification, not a moat finding. [MLQ.ai](https://mlq.ai/news/builderais-collapse-exposes-massive-overestimation-of-sales-to-investors/); [Emergent](https://emergent.sh/learn/builder-ai-reviews)

### Inferences
- A defensibility scoring rubric should separate (a) proprietary data that is refreshed by the product and not obtainable elsewhere, (b) distribution that a model provider cannot replicate, (c) regulatory or licensing barriers, and (d) workflow switching costs. Pure "we use a foundation model" is not defensible by itself. This is inference from the a16z opinion and Builder.ai case, not a measured result.
- Opinion-based frameworks are not evidence that specific moats worked for 2020-2026 cohorts. A proper answer requires cohort-level outcome data that was not retrieved.

### Gaps
- No verified data on which defensibility types correlated with survival or revenue growth for 2020-2026 startups.
- Network effects, switching costs and regulatory-license moat outcomes: no studies retrieved.
- Sequoia, Bessemer, and YC public frameworks on AI moats were not retrieved; they should be treated as opinion when retrieved.

## Key Question 3: For "unserved for structural reasons" problems (regulation, data access, hardware, trust), what is the typical path and time to market, with examples?

### Takeaway
No verified source in this pass documents typical time-to-market or breakthrough examples for regulated, data-access, hardware or trust-constrained categories. The only verified structural-failure examples are from CB Insights and Startup Genome post-mortems (capital, timing, premature scaling), not from regulation-heavy breakthroughs.

### Cited Findings
- CB Insights: "bad timing or macro conditions" drove a disproportionate share of failures in climate and energy, food and agriculture, and blockchain. Examples cited: New Age Meats ($32M) and RECUR ($55M). [CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/)
- CB Insights: Zume ($446M, Series C) pivoted from robot-made pizza to sustainable packaging and still failed to find a viable market. [CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/)
- CB Insights: fintech failures skewed early-stage with median equity of $4M; ZestMoney ($114M) and Ula ($141M) struggled with unit economics as capital dried up. [CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/)
- Startup Genome (2011) lists premature-scaling examples including Webvan and Pets.com (fundraising and customer acquisition ahead of product-market fit) and Cuil and Joost (product). [Startup Genome](https://startupgenome.com/insights/premature-scaling-a-deep-dive)

### Inferences
- Structural-barrier categories (hardware, regulation, trust) appear in the failure data mainly through capital intensity and timing risk. The verified evidence suggests that long build cycles amplify the risk of running out of cash before PMF, which is the framework's main concern for these categories. This is inference, not a measured survival rate.

### Gaps
- Breakthrough examples in regulated fintech, insurance, data-access (e.g., open-banking aggregators), hardware (e.g., EV, space, medtech), and trust (e.g., marketplaces for regulated services) were not retrieved from sources. The notes do not name any such company with a source. Research needed: time from founding to first revenue and to regulatory approval for each.
- No typical time-to-market range is verified.

## Key Question 4: Realistic capital and team requirements for a small founding team to reach first paying customers (software/AI, marketplace, fintech, vertical services, hardware/climate)

### Takeaway
No verified benchmark by category was found. The only verified capital figures are CB Insights's median raised by failed VC-backed companies ($11M) and fintech's median ($4M) in the 2023+ cohort, which describes failed companies, not the required capital for first customers. Do not use these as planning benchmarks.

### Cited Findings
- CB Insights: failed companies in the 2023+ cohort had a median of $11M equity raised before shutdown; fintech median $4M; healthcare and biotech median $47M. [CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/)
- CB Insights: "over half of the companies died within 2 years of their last raise." [CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/)
- Startup Genome (2011): inconsistent (premature-scaling) startups raised about three times as much money and were valued twice as high before scaling as consistent startups. [Startup Genome](https://startupgenome.com/insights/premature-scaling-a-deep-dive)

### Inferences
- The Startup Genome finding that over-raising correlated with premature scaling in 2011 is consistent with the CB Insights capital-cycle findings, so capital raised should be treated as a risk signal, not an input to success.
- Category capital requirements need a planning model with explicit assumptions. This pass did not produce a verified benchmark.

### Gaps
- No verified capital or headcount benchmarks to first paying customer for software/AI, marketplace, fintech, vertical services, or hardware/climate.
- Carta's pre-seed and seed data pages (e.g., [Carta State of Pre-Seed 2025](https://carta.com/data/state-of-pre-seed-2025/)) were surfaced by search but not read; they are the right place to pull round-size medians by stage if needed.

## Key Question 5: What does the evidence say about first-mover advantage vs. fast-follower in recent categories?

### Takeaway
The classic academic test (Golder & Tellis, 1993) directly challenges the pioneer-advantage assumption, but its findings could not be extracted in this pass. No verified evidence on recent (2020-2026) categories was found.

### Cited Findings
- Golder, P. N., & Tellis, G. J. (1993). "Pioneer Advantage: Marketing Logic or Marketing Legend?" *Journal of Marketing Research*, 30(2), 158-170. Title and bibliographic data verified via [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/cf_dev/AbsByAuth.cfm?per_id=634166) and [JSTOR-hosted PDF](https://gtellis.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/pioneering-advantage-marketing-logic-or-marketing-legend.pdf). The SSRN excerpt notes that prior studies (Urban et al. 1986) claimed pioneers have long-lived market share advantages; Golder and Tellis's title frames the question as whether that is logic or legend. **Quantitative findings not verified** (the retrieved PDF text was only the JSTOR header). Some secondary literature cites a pioneer failure rate of about 47%; this is from memory and is NOT verified here, so do not use it without checking the article.

### Inferences
- Treat "first mover advantage" as a hypothesis to test per category, not as a default. The academic literature as titled is skeptical of the simple version.

### Gaps
- Golder & Tellis's sample, findings and pioneer failure rate: not verified (full text not extracted).
- No recent-category (2020-2026) first-mover vs. fast-follower study was retrieved.

## Key Question 6: What signals distinguish a real gap from a fake gap (low willingness to pay, feature vs. product, tiny audience)?

### Takeaway
The best-supported signal in the verified sources is the CB Insights PMF/no-market-need finding and the Startup Genome premature-scaling pattern: companies that scaled spend, team, and fundraising before validating demand did worse. Specific tests for willingness to pay or "feature vs. product" were not verified in a source.

### Cited Findings
- Startup Genome (2011): inconsistent startups spent 2.3 times more likely to be more than one standard deviation above average on customer acquisition, and wrote 3.4 times more lines of code in the discovery phase; 60% of inconsistent startups focused on validating a product in discovery, versus 80% of consistent startups focusing on discovering a problem space. [Startup Genome](https://startupgenome.com/insights/premature-scaling-a-deep-dive)
- Startup Genome: "Building a product without having validated problem/solution fit" and "adding lots of 'nice to have' features" are listed as specific premature-scaling behaviors. [Startup Genome](https://startupgenome.com/insights/premature-scaling-a-deep-dive)
- CB Insights: 72% of companies with 12-month Mosaic data saw their health score decline in the year before death; two-thirds were shrinking in headcount in the 6 months before death; partnership activity dropped 44% from 12-24 months before death to the final 12 months. These are predictive signals for failure, not for gap validity. [CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/)

### Inferences
- A candidate gap should show demand before scaling. Concrete tests inferred from the above (not from a verified source): (1) pre-sold or paid pilots before product build; (2) willingness to pay measured as a price the buyer accepts, not stated interest; (3) repeated use without prompting; (4) an addressable segment large enough for the chosen unit economics. These are practitioner heuristics, not measured thresholds, and should be labeled that way in any scoring document.
- "Feature vs. product" distinction and "tiny group" risk were not directly measured in any retrieved source.

### Gaps
- No verified source on willingness-to-pay tests, feature-vs-product, or niche-size thresholds.

## Scoring Framework (inference and practitioner judgment, not data-backed)

Built from the verified signals above. The weights and scales are the author's proposal and have no empirical calibration from the sources retrieved. Do not present the numbers as benchmarks.

| Dimension | What to evaluate | Evidence in this file |
|---|---|---|
| Demand validation | Paid or pre-committed demand before building; willingness to pay tested at real price | CB Insights PMF 43%; Startup Genome discovery vs. validation pattern |
| Market need vs. feature | Does it solve a workflow that buyers pay to fix, or add a feature to an existing product? | Startup Genome "nice to have" features warning; no direct source |
| Unit economics | Gross margin and CAC payback at target price; avoid spending ahead of economics | CB Insights unit economics 19%; Startup Genome customer-acquisition overspend |
| Capital intensity and timing | Capital to first paying customer; time to revenue; exposure to macro cycles | CB Insights bad timing 29%; median $11M raised by failures; no category benchmark |
| Distribution | Channel the team can reach customers through; cost of acquisition | Startup Genome customer-dimension premature scaling; no benchmark |
| Defensibility | Proprietary data refreshed by product, switching costs, regulatory license, network effects | a16z opinion; Builder.ai as diligence caution; no measured effect |
| Team and scaling discipline | Hiring and fundraising sequenced after demand is proven | Startup Genome team/fundraising premature-scaling findings (2011) |
| Regulatory and structural barriers | Time to licensing, data access, hardware cycles | No verified sources; gap |

Practitioner note (opinion): frameworks from a16z, Sequoia, Bessemer, and YC were not retrieved in this pass and should be added with their opinion labels before use.
